# 03 — Brand Psychology and Behavioural Evidence

## No deterministic colour myths
A rigorous design practice does not assume “red makes people purchase,” “blue guarantees trust,” “green increases wealth,” or “round logos are always friendly.” A large systematic review published in 2025 synthesised 132 peer-reviewed colour-emotion studies covering 42,266 participants across 64 countries. It found recurring many-to-many associations involving hue, brightness and saturation, while warning that abstract associations do not prove the same effect on emotion or behaviour in a specific context. This is strong reason to avoid simplistic psychology tables.

Primary evidence: [Jonauskaitė and Möhr systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39806242/). Cross-cultural marketing review: [Aslam](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13527260500247827).

## Perceptual attention
Contrast, scale, position, whitespace, motion and novelty influence which item is noticed. Use attention to support tasks. Making a decorative element more salient than a Buy/Wait status is an error. Salience should be intentional: reserve extreme contrast for primary decisions, exceptions or warnings; maintain calm for background structures.

## Familiarity and processing fluency
Users learn expected conventions. A primary navigation sidebar is not the right place for radical novelty; distinctive brand identity can live in mark, type, imagery, motion quality and content. Familiarity can make interactions easier, but an application that hides every advanced control in the name of “simplicity” may harm experts. Discoverable defaults plus optional deeper controls often outperform either extreme.

## The aesthetic-usability effect
Attractive interfaces may be perceived as easier and can make people forgiving of small problems. The effect can also conceal actual friction in tests: praise for the visual style does not prove people completed the task quickly or accurately. The Nielsen Norman Group review distinguishes aesthetic perception from functional performance. Test both.
Source: [NN/g aesthetic-usability effect](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/).

## Brand salience and attribution
Distinctive assets matter when people connect them to the **right brand**. An attractive new logo can be memorable yet associated with the wrong company. Record:
- unprompted attribution: what brand does an isolated cue bring to mind?;
- prompted recognition among realistic competitors;
- uniqueness: does the cue also remind users of a competitor?;
- repeated exposure and contextual recognition;
- category fit without blending into generic styling.

Do not claim these scores without a real participant study. The Ehrenberg-Bass research tradition distinguishes prevalence and uniqueness as elements of asset strength. Source: [Ehrenberg-Bass distinctive assets](https://assets.marketingscience.info/annual-report-2009.pdf).

## Gestalt and visual organization
Proximity, grouping, enclosure, similarity, continuity and figure-ground make relationships easier to infer. Apply them carefully: a cyan label paired with a red icon might look like one warning if placed inside a tight border. Combine semantic meaning with placement, icon, wording and predictable geometry. Distinctive layout rhythm should never destroy comprehension.

## Mental models and error prevention
Users imagine how a system works based on prior experience. A chart labelled “Live” signals real-time data; an old quote presented without a timestamp is misleading. Use explicit state vocabulary: connecting, updated, delayed, cached, demo, historical, error, no setup. For destructive workflows, make reversibility and confirmation clear. People should not need to infer hidden state from subtle animation.

## First impressions versus real use
A brief exposure test can tell you what someone notices first or what category they think a product belongs to. It does not show reliable task completion or conversion. NN/g recommends separating quick first-impression exercises from real usability tasks and examining behaviour, not only post-test compliments.
Source: [NN/g first-impressions research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-impressions-human-automaticity/).

## Colour selection as context-specific hypothesis
Choose palette with four tests: category fit, competitive distinctiveness, accessible contrast and real production behaviour. A vivid orange may be energetic in a youth event but overwhelming in a high-density finance terminal. Black can feel elegant or oppressive depending on typography, white space, imagery and content. Do not claim a general emotional association predicts a purchase.

## Ethical persuasion
Trust must be earned through accuracy and transparency. Never fabricate scarcity, fake countdowns, guaranteed trading outcomes, testimonials, followers, performance data or security claims. Do not make accepting personal-data collection easier than refusing it. Make important prices, privacy, cancellation and subscription terms understandable. Psychological insight should help people make informed decisions, not exploit their vulnerabilities.

## Cognitive load
Not every reduction in visible choices reduces difficulty. Too many equal-weight options can confuse, but hiding advanced controls behind several layers can raise effort for experts. Classify features by task frequency, risk and complexity. Expose critical status and action; progressively reveal rare settings. If a compact UI trades screen space for discoverability, offer titles/tooltips, keyboard access and easy restore actions.

## Brand psychology experiment worksheet
For any proposed cue record: hypothesis, mechanism, intended audience, context, visual execution, evidence source, predicted observable change, potential harm/confound, test procedure, observed result and revision. Example: “A stable sidebar makes timeframe selection faster” should be tested by having users switch timeframes with both sidebar layouts. It is not a truth merely because GitHub or another product uses sidebars.

## Specific tests for logos
- Distinction: place mark among competitors in greyscale and colour.
- Legibility: ask actual people to read the name at 24/48/120 pixels.
- Recognition: show symbol without wordmark; note false attribution.
- Preference: ask what impression it conveys, then separate taste from recognition.
- Reproduction: test at printer limits rather than judging a retina screen alone.
- Culture: seek local interpretations of contested symbols and words.

## Bias checklist
Beware confirmation bias (“I designed it so users will like it”); sunk-cost fallacy (protecting an elaborate logo despite failure); halo effects (beauty masks usability); authority bias (copying large-brand UI without context); survivorship bias (only studying polished launches); and data-free universal psychological claims.

## High-stakes applications
Finance, health, government and job-related interfaces especially require accurate status, safe error handling, clear content and disclosure. Aesthetic authority can mislead: a trading signal must not look like an executed order. Risk can be communicated through type, position and iconography in addition to colour, and should remain legible to colour-blind users.

## Cultural context
Associations vary across countries, subcultures, generations, industries and circumstances. Cultural research should involve actual people and relevant sources; do not convert an ethnic or regional identity into generic motifs. A Xitsonga brand can express meaning through voice, story, craft, music and visual structure while remaining contemporary and specific.

## Practical evidence hierarchy
Normative accessibility requirement > broad peer-reviewed research for general perceptual association > local user research for intended audience > direct user-task observation > practitioner experience > creative hypothesis. These are different types of evidence; some local qualitative observations may be more relevant than a broad but unrelated experiment. State limitations honestly.
