# 16 — Print, Merchandise, Packaging and Production Engineering

## The final medium sets constraints
A flat-screen mockup cannot prove an identity works on fabric, cardboard, signage, engraved metal, embroidery or cheap digital printing. Design for the actual manufacturing process, vendor capabilities, viewing distance, ink limits, substrate and quantity. A logo that relies on transparent glow can disappear during screen printing; a hairline can break during cutting or stitching.

## Physical design brief
Capture:
- item type and dimensions;
- substrate/material;
- vendor and production method;
- ink/spot colour limits, gradient support and accepted profiles;
- minimum stroke and gap sizes;
- bleed, trim, fold, dieline and safe zone requirements;
- quantity, sample method and budget;
- wearing/handling conditions;
- expected viewing distance;
- care labels or legally required information;
- available colour proofs and official brand references;
- shipping, storage and durability constraints.

Never make up a universal print tolerance. Ask the actual printer for their specifications and test physical samples.

## Colour management
Web RGB colours do not have an exact guaranteed CMYK equivalent. Identify whether the job needs CMYK, spot colours (including Pantone where available), fabric inks or sublimation. Use ICC colour profiles and approved proof conditions where professional workflows justify them. Screen colours may look noticeably different on brown cardboard, black cotton and glossy vinyl. Define acceptable approximations in a print-specific palette.

## Artwork preparation
Keep vector artwork for logo and scalable line designs. Text may require outlined versions for compatible vendor workflows but preserve editable source. Avoid unnecessary rasterization. Confirm transparent backgrounds are genuinely transparent (not checkerboard pixels), and that placed artwork's alpha compositing works on both light and dark substrates.

Define output requirements: PDF/X variant when requested, SVG/EPS/PDF vector as supported, layer separations, bleed, crop marks, dieline lock, overprint rules and resolution for raster imagery. Do not export one generic file for all vendor types.

## Apparel design
Screen printing, DTG, DTF, sublimation, embroidery and vinyl have different strengths:
- Screen printing favours planned spot colours and durable separations at volume.
- DTG and DTF can support complexity but depend on garment/fabric and transfer quality.
- Embroidery needs simplified forms, safe gaps, sufficient stitch thickness and minimal micro-details.
- Vinyl cutting dislikes fragile islands and intricate fine corners.
- Sublimation depends heavily on suitable substrate and garment colour.
Choose a method based on desired look, run size, cost and garment type. Consult vendor proof before production.

### FENRY-specific caution
If a FENRY apparel line has an explicit maximum of six print colours and rejects gradients, detailed photos or thin lines, that constraint outranks a fashionable design guide. Create bold vector silhouettes, proportioned type and strong one-colour proofs. Test realistic chest print and back print sizes. Keep approved brand character and avoid adding violent motifs when the current capsule forbids them.

## Packaging systems
Package surfaces have multiple jobs: brand identification, product information, hierarchy, legal content, opening/use guidance and shelf or shipping durability. Design front, back, side, interior and unboxing only as needed. A beautiful front label that hides ingredients, weight, contact and instructions is not production-ready. Use actual regulatory text when applicable; ask a qualified expert rather than inventing compliance.

## Signage and environmental branding
Evaluate legibility by distance, lighting, weather, viewing angle, material texture and competing visual noise. Establish hierarchies for name, category, location and action. A logo may need a simplified high-contrast variant for small backlit signs. Signage colours should be tested at the real location rather than in an ideal studio render.

## Co-branding on physical assets
A media collaboration poster, event banner or merchandise may carry multiple sponsors. Document hierarchy and approved lockup spacing. Obtain original vector assets; do not redraw a partner's logo from a blurry screenshot. Verify each brand's permitted use and colour treatment. Use a neutral separator if brand palettes conflict.

## Print sample acceptance
Test a sample at the actual size. Ask:
- Are thin strokes intact?
- Are small letters readable?
- Are coloured surfaces consistent enough?
- Did gradients or photos reproduce acceptably?
- Are registration errors noticeable?
- Do fabric folds distort key lettering?
- Are margins/bleeds safe?
- Does the package actually fold/close correctly?
- Are barcodes/QR codes scannable under real conditions?
- Are rights, regulations and credits correct?

## Sustainable design as a tradeoff
Material sustainability requires material, manufacturing, usage and disposal context; no universally “green” texture or recycled-paper visual proves environmental benefit. Avoid claiming eco-friendliness without evidence. If sustainability is a goal, document verified materials, wastage reductions and supplier practices, then design around them.

## Vendor handoff checklist
Provide correct version, purpose, quantities, dimensions, approved colour separations, master vector file, raster resolution, dieline, bleed/safe area, exact logos, typographic treatments, prohibited effects, mockup for orientation, licensing, and signoff record. Store vendor proof separately from final production-ready files.

## Typical failure modes
Mockups-only; incorrect CMYK conversion; fake transparency; unlicensed type; a graphic too fine for vinyl or stitches; text on the fold; too many colours for allowed print; logo with unreadable tagline; poor partner lockups; old brand logo accidentally used; low-resolution embedded pictures; no actual sample inspection.
